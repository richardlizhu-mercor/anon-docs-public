Data Acquisition · Anonymization

Human Evals

Human-in-the-loop evaluation across the anonymization pipeline. Moderators review real anonymized data in the Studio annotation UI to score detection precision/recall, identity-resolution clustering (plus a knowledge-graph stretch), and faker quality.

Status: Draft apps/server/data-acq Studio annotation UI 2026-07

Summary

Put a trained human on every stage of the pipeline, and turn what they label into a feedback loop.

Automated metrics only take us so far. The ground truth we score against is imperfect, faker realism has no automated measure today, and what counts as a "correct" cluster is ultimately a human call, since over-merging two people is a privacy leak. Human review gives an honest read on quality and, over time, the labeled data to train against.

Why we're doing this

Four goals, ordered by importance rather than timeline. The near-term steps at the bottom are how we earn the top one.

  1. Long-termClose the feedback loop. Turn human labels into training data and train models for higher anonymization quality. This is the point of the whole exercise.
  2. Mid-termAssess our current anonymization quality. Get an honest, unbiased read on how good the pipeline is today.
  3. Short-termTrain the experts and work out the kinks of task generation on RL Studio, so the annotation process itself is reliable.
  4. Most short-termCorrect the data samples we have right now. Fix the labels and anonymization on the samples already in hand.

Where eval error comes from

When a metric says the pipeline is wrong, it's one of three things. Only the third is real model error. The first two are error in the measuring instrument, and they're ours to remove, so the number we report reflects anonymization quality and nothing else.

SOURCE 1

Moderator misalignment

The moderator is wrong: undertrained, inconsistent, or mistaken. This is label noise, not a pipeline defect.

Fix: gold tasks and adjudication surface the mislabels; feed them back into moderator training.

SOURCE 2

Label / objective misalignment

We're scoring against something we didn't fully label, or we're not yet sure what we want to measure.

Fix — the eval flywheel: have moderators label a superset of everything we might ever want to evaluate, then a post-processing layer filters that down to the metric of the moment. We can change the metric without re-labeling.

SOURCE 3

Genuine anonymization error

The pipeline actually got it wrong: a missed PII span, an over-merge, an unrealistic fake.

This is the signal we want. Everything above exists to strip sources 1 and 2 out so this is what's left.

1 · Detection — precision & recall

Did the pipeline flag the right spans as PII, and did it catch all of them?

Each detected span starts Suggested. The moderator Confirms the ones that are real PII, Rejects the ones that aren't, and Manually marks any the detector missed. Those three actions are exactly the ground truth the offline eval needs:

LabelMeansCounts as
Confirmedreal PII the model foundtrue positive
Rejectedflagged but not PIIfalse positive
Manually markedreal PII the model missedfalse negative

precision = Confirmed / (Confirmed + Rejected) and recall = Confirmed / (Confirmed + Manually marked), with F1 the harmonic mean, broken down by the 20-type taxonomy. The moderator's adjudication is the ground truth, so it replaces the model-built truth the automated eval otherwise scores against.

Target: ~95% F1 is a reasonable ceiling Published NER systems top out around here, so it's a sensible upper bound to aim for before any pipeline-specific work confirms it. We can trade precision for recall or vice versa; F1 ≈ 0.95 is the goal.
Entity typePrecisionRecall
PER (person)~0.96~0.95
LOC (location)~0.92~0.93
ORG (organization)~0.88~0.91
SOTA reference. ORG is the hardest type everywhere, which matches our own weak spot: Brand/Org are where our detector lags most.
A second objective: measure the moderators themselves Once the experts are trained, we score their own labels — against an adjudicated gold set, and via inter-annotator agreement — to get a human precision / recall / F1. Today "humans are good at this" is an assertion, not a number; this makes it measurable. My expectation is the human F1 lands at or below SOTA model performance, which is a useful ceiling check in itself.

2 · Identity Resolution — entity clustering

Are the values that refer to the same real entity grouped together, and are different entities kept apart?

The Clusters tab shows every detected entity in an unassigned list on the left and the pipeline's proposed clusters on the right. The moderator drags each entity into the cluster for the real identity it belongs to, spins up a + New cluster when something doesn't fit, and pulls an entity back out when it was grouped wrong. One cluster is one real entity and all of its aliases — names, emails, usernames. For example, identity_001 gathers Marissa Rose Saez, Marissa S, Marissa-Saez, marissa@allcitynetwork.com, marissa233, and marissaez11 into one person. (Tabs: Compare · Clusters · Consolidate · Export; the moderator hits Submit for Review when done.)

Every assignment is, in scoring terms, a merge or a split — the two error classes that matter, and they map straight onto the metric:

Over every pair of aliasesCounts as
both say same entity, and they aretrue positive
pipeline merged them, but they're different (a split fixes this)false positive — over-merge
pipeline split them, but they're the same (a merge fixes this)false negative — under-merge

The metric is pairwise clustering F1, reported as a coverage-adjusted number so missed aliases count against it. Precision is the one to watch: an over-merge means two real people share a fake identity, which is a privacy leak, so we bias toward keeping entities apart. This human-labeled cluster set is the ground truth the IR merger is trained and scored against.

2.1 · Knowledge-graph clustering — stretch

Stretch goal Beyond "which values are the same entity," have moderators link distinct entities into typed relationships — who belongs to which organization, who authored what — the edges of an activity / knowledge graph. That produces the ground truth for the EAG's relationship features and eventual cross-modal IR. It's a genuine stretch: there's no relationship-annotation surface or graph metric today, so it needs new tooling (a graph-annotation view plus a graph-eval metric) before it can run.

3 · Faker quality

Are the fakes realistic, type-faithful, and internally consistent?

Faker is the best-covered stage today. Our existing RL Studio review already captures the consistency and coherency signal, and we can already run quality analyses on top of the anonymized output.

Consistency & coherency — already covered by RL Studio

The existing edit-anonymization step in RL Studio is sufficient here; we don't need new tooling to get consistency and coherency data. Next to the Original / Anonymized compare view, each entity's original → fake mapping is listed with a checkbox — the moderator accepts the fake or edits it inline to override, and can add variants so alternate surface forms of one identity share the same fake (e.g. Netflix → Arcpin, Nikhil → Skye, Neetu → Eloise). The keep rate, how often our fake is accepted unchanged, is a direct realism metric, and every override is a labeled example of a fake that wasn't good enough. Moderators also catch format breaks and persona inconsistencies: a name, email, and username that don't cohere, or an org that no longer reads as an org. The offline complement is the name-collision rate — two different real people mapped to the same fake.

Consistency (metric): the share of reviewed clusters with no consistency violation — one real entity maps to one fake everywhere, and the cluster's name, email, and username cohere. Each mismatch a moderator flags in the edit step is a labeled failure we can track and train on.

Type preservation — an example of what we can measure

Charis ran the analysis below. Take a fixed NER reader, run it over the raw text and over the anonymized text, and compare the per-type entity distributions per service. When the two diverge, the faker is substituting values that get re-typed to a different entity class — a realism tell. The clearest case is ramp: real org and vendor names (read as ORG, 43%) get replaced with fakes the reader sees as places, so GPE jumps from 5% to 25% — fake companies read as geography.

allcity — entity-type mix, raw vs anonymized, per service (analysis by Charis). Big raw→anon gaps (e.g. ramp ORG→GPE) mark fakes that get re-typed; small gaps mean type is preserved.

This is a one-off analysis today; making an NER-based type-preservation metric a standing check is the natural follow-up.
