Data Acquisition · Anonymization

Identity Resolution, rebuilt — a plain-language overview

How we make sure one real person becomes one consistent fake across an entire dataset — and why the new engine is both safer and more accurate as datasets grow.

High-level overview Anonymization · Stage 2 Updated July 2026
In one paragraph

We anonymize customer data by replacing every real name, email, and identifier with a realistic fake. Identity Resolution is the step that figures out which of those values refer to the same real person or company, so each one gets a single, consistent fake everywhere. We replaced a hand-tuned, one-size-fits-all method with a trained model that judges each pair on its own merits and learns its own settings. The payoff: far fewer privacy-risky mistakes, and — unlike the old system — it gets more accurate as datasets get bigger.

What it does

Picture a data export where the same employee shows up as John Doe, j.doe@acme.com, jdoe, and @johnd. If we anonymized each one independently, John would turn into four different fake people and the data would stop making sense. Identity Resolution groups those four back into one person, so they receive one fake identity — consistently, everywhere the data references them. It does the same for companies, phone numbers, addresses, and every other kind of sensitive value.

The one trade-off that governs everything

One kind of mistake is far worse than the other. Merging two different people means they share a fake identity — a privacy leak. Splitting one person into two fakes is only a cosmetic flaw. So the system is deliberately cautious about merging: when in doubt, keep them apart.

What changed

Before

We scored how similar two values looked using a fixed recipe of text-matching rules, poured everything into one big similarity graph, and split it into groups with a single algorithm whose knobs we tuned by hand.

It worked — but the knobs were educated guesses, and it actually got less accurate as datasets grew larger.

After

Now a trained model looks at each candidate pair and answers one question — "same entity, or not?" — weighing many signals: not just how the text looks, but who appears alongside whom in the data.

It learns its own thresholds from labeled examples instead of us hand-picking them, then groups the confirmed matches carefully, with hard rules that block obvious errors (e.g., two people with different last names never merge).

And it makes far fewer of the dangerous mistakes

Of the merges it actually makes, the new engine is right 96–97% of the time, versus 75–86% before. Because a wrong merge is the privacy-risky failure, that jump matters more than the headline accuracy number: it means roughly 3–4× fewer wrong merges.

Measured on internal, synthetic benchmark datasets; numbers are directional, not a production guarantee.

Why it improves with scale

The old engine leaned on how text looks, so as a dataset grows and more look-alike names appear, it makes more accidental merges — accuracy drifts down. The new engine also uses context — who shows up together in the same records — and it tunes itself to be cautious. More data gives it more context to work with, so it finds genuine matches without loosening its guard. That's why it gets better, not worse, at scale.

Where it's headed

  • Connect the dots across documents too, not just spreadsheet-style data — so a person mentioned in an email and in a CRM resolve to the same identity.
  • Use richer "who worked with whom" signals from across services to sharpen the hardest calls.
  • Upgrade the underlying model as we collect more labeled examples — the current design is built to swap in a stronger model without rework.
